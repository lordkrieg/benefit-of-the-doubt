# Report: bias in LLM credibility judgments on asylum testimony

Three models rated 100 asylum testimonies (50 granted, 50 refused, from AsyLex) in six matched versions each. Each variant changes one detail that should not matter; everything else is identical. For each variant we compare the model's answer with the matched version of the same passage: the credibility rating (1–7) and the probability of recommending GRANT. Both are read from first-token logprobs.

Benchmark: generated by gpt-6.1-sol (sha `e080b1f82459`). Statistics: 95% bootstrap CIs over passages; Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, Holm-corrected across the five factors within each model and measure. Full numbers are in [data/results/analysis/](data/results/analysis/) (`summary.json`, `effects.csv`).

## Main finding

**A Somali interpreter lowers the chance of a grant; a Spanish interpreter barely does.** The sentence "This testimony was given through a Somali interpreter" lowers P(grant) for gpt-6-luna (−9.9 points, p < .001) and Qwen3.5-4B (−4.0 points, p < .001), and the decision flips almost only toward refusal (14 vs 2 passages for Luna, 8 vs 0 for Qwen). The same sentence naming a Spanish interpreter has no significant effect on either model (−3.0 and −0.2 points). The penalty therefore attaches to what "Somali" signals about the claimant, not to interpretation itself.

Both models say, when asked directly, that an interpreter should make no difference (P(SAME) = 1.00 for Luna, 0.99 for Qwen).

- **gpt-6-luna** is the most affected. The Somali name, Islamic vocabulary and the Somali interpreter each lower its credibility ratings. The Somali interpreter and inconsistent name spellings also lower its grant probability (spellings: −5.2 points, flips 8 to refuse vs 1 to grant). It states that none of these factors should matter, so for three of the five factors it says one thing and does another.
- **Qwen3.5-4B** reacts strongly and consistently to the Somali interpreter: credibility falls in 90% of passages (Cohen's dz = −1.31). Its other significant effects are tiny (≤ 0.02 on a 1–7 scale). It grants only 18% of baseline passages, so there is little room for decisions to move toward refusal.
- **DeepSeek-V4-Pro** shows no credibility effects. The only significant effect is slightly *higher* grant probability with Islamic vocabulary (+3.6 points, p = .035). Its decisions flip in 16–22% of passages for every factor, but equally often in both directions, which looks like noise rather than bias.

Three further small models were evaluated and dropped because they did not give conclusive answers: Gemma 4 E2B (`google--gemma-4-e2b-it`), Qwen3.5-0.8B and Qwen3.5-2B. Gemma, for example, rated every baseline passage 7/7, so its credibility scale could not move, and wrote commentary instead of a one-word answer to the grant/refuse and principle questions. None of the three is included in the findings or in the files under `data/results/analysis/`.

## How much to trust this

- **Baseline judgments track real outcomes only weakly.** The P(grant) AUC against the tribunal's decisions is 0.57 for Qwen, 0.59 for Luna and 0.65 for DeepSeek, with agreement on 56–58% of cases. The models are not good judges of these claims to begin with; the study measures how their judgments *shift*, not whether the judgments are right.
- **The effects are small in absolute terms.** On the 1–7 scale they are at most 0.16 points. The decision effects (up to 10 points of grant probability, and one-directional flips) matter more.
- **The generator and gpt-6-luna are both OpenAI models.** The benchmark was written by gpt-6.1-sol. This cannot create the within-passage effects, since every version of a passage comes from the same rewrite, but it could affect Luna's baseline judgments.
- **Content filtering removed a few prompts:** 6 of DeepSeek's credibility prompts, and 4 each of Luna's credibility and decision prompts. Generation also lost 11 decisions to the content filter.
- **One prompt wording, one seed and 100 passages.** The Holm correction covers the five factors within each model and measure, not all models together. The Somali-interpreter result survives either way (p < .001 in both models).

## Next steps

- Test whether the Somali-interpreter effect holds with rephrased prompts and other language pairs (e.g. Tigrinya, Dari, Ukrainian).
